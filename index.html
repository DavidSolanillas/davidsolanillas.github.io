Research

Contact models and robot learning

If a simulator can't reproduce a contact behavior, a robot trained in it can't learn to rely on that behavior either. I traced three cases where MuJoCo's contact model breaks down to a specific modelling choice, not to the RL on top of it.

Lifting with friction alone. Shirai et al., NeurIPS 2025 workshop found that raising the friction coefficient wasn't enough to reproduce a friction-only lift in simulation, and added a virtual wall behind the object to make the task trainable. Our reading: their convex contact model can't represent stick-slip transitions or pure sticking. A bristle friction model lifts the same object with no wall.

Sustaining a jam under load. Tilt a brick until it wedges, then increase the load: reproducing this in MuJoCo takes heavy parameter tuning, because dropping the complementarity condition makes the normal force follow from a penalty stiffness, and the defaults never reach the value needed to hold the wedge. In our LCP-based model, sticking is a real state — force at zero slip velocity — so the wedge survives up to 100,000 N.

Recovering a grasp after partial release. Grasp an object, partly release it so it slips, then recover the grasp. Velocity-level formulations need heavy tuning here too: without an elastic state, exact sticking is never attained, some slip always persists, and the grasp is never quite recovered. In the bristle model, friction force is non-zero at zero slip velocity, so the grasp recovers cleanly.

Same pattern each time: the gap wasn't in the policy, it was in what the simulator could represent in the first place.

A point-mass representation for rigid-body dynamics

Every rigid body can be represented as a small system of point masses instead of the usual inertia tensor. The mass matrix comes out constant, the nonlinear inertial terms disappear, and the constraint Jacobians are linear in the generalized coordinates. That matters for differentiable physics in particular: the terms that are normally expensive to differentiate through a physics engine either vanish or can be precomputed once, instead of on every step.

Particle-based simulation of rigid bodies and joints

At CM Labs Simulations I implement and validate this in C++ inside Vortex Studio, their production physics engine. It builds on the Parallel Particles (P²) method (Holz, VRIPHYS 2014), originally designed for soil simulation and built for fast, parallel contact detection between free particles. I hold particles together with constant-distance constraints to form rigid bodies — the original method only has loose particles — so a structure like a building can be modelled as clusters of constrained particles while keeping the method's fast contact detection, which is what makes large-scale demolition and fragmentation tractable. Because soil and bodies live in the same particle framework, the same approach also handles bodies buried in soil.

I'm extending the same approach to multibody systems with joints — revolute and prismatic so far — validated first in MATLAB before anything goes into C++.
